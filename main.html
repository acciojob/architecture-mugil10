A field guide to

Architecture

From load-bearing stone to load-bearing glass — how buildings have carried the weight of human ambition across three thousand years.

Classical Foundations

Ancient builders in Greece and Rome worked out the grammar that architecture still speaks today: the column, the arch, and the dome. Proportion was treated as a moral question as much as a structural one — a building was expected to look as sound as it stood. Marble and limestone did the heavy lifting, quite literally, and the orders of Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian columns became a vocabulary that architects would keep quoting for centuries after Rome fell.

The Modern Turn

Steel frames and reinforced concrete changed what a wall was for. By the early twentieth century, a building no longer needed thick masonry to hold itself up, so glass could take over entire facades and open the inside to daylight. Architects like Le Corbusier and Mies van der Rohe pushed this new freedom toward clean lines and open floor plans, trading ornament for honesty of structure — a shift whose skyline is still rising in every major city today.
